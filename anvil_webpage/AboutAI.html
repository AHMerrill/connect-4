Building AI for Connect 4

A deep dive into training neural networks to master a classic game

🎯 The Challenge

Connect 4 is a solved game — with perfect play, the first player can always force a win. But finding that perfect play requires analyzing millions of possible board positions. Our goal was to train neural networks that could learn to play at a high level by studying expert moves, without explicitly programming game strategy.

We built two different neural network architectures — a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and a Transformer — and compared their performance. Both networks treat the problem as image classification with 7 categories, where each category represents a column to drop a chip.

📊 Training Data: Learning from MCTS

Instead of hand-coding game strategies, we trained our networks on moves generated by Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) — the same algorithm used by AlphaGo and AlphaZero. MCTS simulates thousands of random games from each position to find statistically strong moves.

Our dataset contains 703,111 positions from MCTS with 2000 simulations per move. After applying horizontal mirroring (exploiting the board's symmetry), we have 1.4M+ training examples.

Board Encoding

Each board position is encoded as a 6×7×2 tensor:

  • Channel 0: 1 where Player 1 (orange) has a chip, 0 elsewhere
  • Channel 1: 1 where Player 2 (white) has a chip, 0 elsewhere

This two-channel representation lets the network clearly distinguish between the two players' positions.

Why Two Heads?

Both networks output two predictions:

  • Policy Head: A probability distribution over the 7 columns — which move to play
  • Value Head: A single number (-1 to +1) predicting the game outcome from this position

🔍 Board Analysis: Easy vs. Hard Positions

Not all positions are equally easy to evaluate. Here's an analysis of where our models succeed and struggle:

✓ Easy: Obvious Win
98% Confidence
Why it's easy: Three orange chips in a row with an open fourth slot. The network recognizes this winning pattern instantly — it's exactly what convolutions excel at detecting.
✓ Easy: Block Opponent
95% Confidence
Why it's easy: Opponent has three in a row and must be blocked immediately. Defensive patterns like this appear frequently in training data.
✗ Hard: Complex Mid-Game
42% Confidence
Why it's hard: Multiple viable moves exist with similar long-term value. The network must reason about threats several moves ahead — something that requires deep search, not pattern matching.
✗ Hard: Dual Threats
38% Confidence
Why it's hard: Setting up a "fork" (two winning threats at once) requires planning ahead. MCTS finds these with extensive search, but neural networks must learn the abstract pattern.

Key Insights from Our Analysis

  • Immediate threats are easy: Both networks excel at recognizing "three in a row" patterns that require immediate action.
  • Early game is noisy: Opening moves have similar values, so the network's confidence is lower even when it picks the "correct" move.
  • Diagonal threats are trickier: Horizontal and vertical patterns are more frequently encountered; diagonal attacks sometimes get missed.
  • Strategic positions are hardest: Positions where the best move isn't about immediate threats but long-term positioning challenge the network.
  • Center control matters: The network learned that controlling the center column leads to more winning opportunities — an emergent strategy!

🤔 Why Some Boards Are Classified Poorly

Our neural networks sometimes make mistakes. Here's why:

1. Limited Look-Ahead

MCTS runs 2000 simulations to evaluate each move. Our networks make instant predictions based on patterns they've memorized. Complex positions requiring 5+ move look-ahead often confuse the network.

2. Training Data Bias

Our training data comes from MCTS playing against itself. Certain positions that arise from human mistakes may not be well-represented, causing poor predictions on those boards.

3. The "Equally Good" Problem

Sometimes multiple moves are equally strong. When MCTS gives 30% probability to three different moves, the network struggles to pick a clear winner. Low confidence doesn't mean a bad move — it might mean several good moves exist.

4. Rare Tactical Patterns

Unusual tactical motifs (like setting up a simultaneous double-threat) appear rarely in training. The network may not have seen enough examples to learn these patterns reliably.

🏆 Conclusion

Both our CNN and Transformer achieve ~73% accuracy at predicting MCTS moves and ~90% top-2 accuracy. They play at a strong level, easily beating casual players and providing a challenging opponent.

The key takeaway: neural networks can learn to play Connect 4 at a high level by studying expert moves, but they remain imperfect. The game's complexity — with its long-term planning requirements — still poses challenges for pure pattern-matching approaches.
