Model Report

Training Neural Networks to Play Connect 4

🎯 The Challenge

Connect 4 is a solved game — with perfect play, the first player can always force a win. But finding that perfect play requires analyzing millions of possible board positions.

Our goal was to train neural networks that could learn to play at a high level by studying expert moves, without explicitly programming game strategy. We built two different architectures — a CNN and a Transformer — and compared their performance.

Key Approach
Treat the board as an image (6×7 pixels with 2 channels) and classify which of 7 columns is the best move.
🧠 CNN Model
  • AlphaZero-style residual network
  • 10 residual blocks, 256 filters
  • ~72% top-1 accuracy
  • Pattern-based recognition
⚡ Transformer Model
  • Vision Transformer (ViT) style
  • 6 layers, 8 attention heads
  • ~74% top-1 accuracy
  • Attention-based relationships

📊 Training Data Generation

Instead of hand-coding game strategies, we trained our networks on moves generated by Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) — the same algorithm used by AlphaGo and AlphaZero.

MCTS simulates thousands of random games from each position to find statistically strong moves. We used 2000 simulations per move to generate high-quality training data.

🎲 Diverse Game Coverage

To ensure diverse coverage across early, mid, and late-game positions, we varied the number of random opening moves from 4 to 14 using a right-tailed probability distribution.

This approach creates more even coverage across different game states, preventing the model from overfitting to common opening patterns while still learning strong mid and late-game play.

📦 Dataset Statistics
  • Raw positions: 703,111
  • After mirroring: 1,406,222
  • MCTS simulations: 2000/move
  • Board encoding: 6×7×2 tensor
🔄 Data Augmentation
  • Horizontal mirroring: Doubles dataset
  • Move-depth weighting: Balances game stages
  • Random openings: 4-14 moves (right-tailed)
  • No duplicates: Unique positions only

🎛️ Board Encoding

Each board position is encoded as a 6×7×2 tensor:

  • Channel 0: 1 where Player 1 (orange) has a chip, 0 elsewhere
  • Channel 1: 1 where Player 2 (white) has a chip, 0 elsewhere

This two-channel representation lets the network clearly distinguish between the two players' positions, similar to how image classification uses RGB channels.

🧠 CNN Architecture

An AlphaZero-style residual CNN that treats the board like an image. Convolutional layers scan for spatial patterns like lines, threats, and blocking opportunities.

Key Insight
Both models predict the exact MCTS move ~73% of the time, and their top-2 predictions include the best move ~90% of the time — impressive given MCTS runs 2000 simulations while our networks make instant predictions!

⚖️ Model Comparison

  • CNN strengths: Faster inference, better at recognizing local patterns (3-in-a-row threats)
  • Transformer strengths: Slightly higher accuracy, better at long-range dependencies
  • Trade-off: Transformer takes longer to train but achieves marginally better results
  • In practice: Both models play at a similar level against humans

🔍 Easy vs. Hard Positions

Not all positions are equally easy to evaluate. Here's an analysis of where our models succeed and struggle:

✓ Easy: Obvious Win
98% Confidence
Why it's easy: Three orange chips in a row with an open fourth slot. Classic winning pattern that appears frequently in training.
✓ Easy: Block Opponent
95% Confidence
Why it's easy: Opponent has three in a row and must be blocked immediately. Defensive patterns are well-learned.
✗ Hard: Complex Mid-Game
42% Confidence
Why it's hard: Multiple viable moves exist. Requires reasoning about threats several moves ahead.
✗ Hard: Fork Setup
38% Confidence
Why it's hard: Setting up a "fork" (two winning threats) requires planning ahead — hard to learn from patterns alone.

💡 Key Insights

  • Immediate threats are easy: Both networks excel at recognizing "three in a row" patterns.
  • Early game is noisy: Opening moves have similar values, so confidence is lower.
  • Diagonal threats are trickier: Horizontal/vertical patterns are more common in training.
  • Strategic positions are hardest: Positions requiring long-term planning challenge the networks.
  • Center control emerges: The network learned that controlling center leads to more wins — an emergent strategy!

🤔 Why Some Boards Fail

  • Limited look-ahead: MCTS runs 2000 simulations; our networks make instant predictions based on patterns.
  • Training data bias: Some rare tactical positions may not be well-represented in training.
  • "Equally good" problem: When multiple moves are similar, low confidence doesn't mean a bad move.
  • Rare tactical patterns: Unusual motifs (like double-threats) appear rarely and are harder to learn.
